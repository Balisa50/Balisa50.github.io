Standard mortality models are calibrated on American and British data, and they carry that calibration into places it does not belong. They under-represent the constant background hazard that matters in Sub-Saharan Africa, malaria, road traffic, infectious disease, and over-represent an old-age mortality pattern that a much younger population does not have.

So this is a full life insurance risk model built for the region: mortality fitted from first principles, survival analysis with censoring handled properly, premium pricing, and value at risk from a Monte Carlo simulation with a pandemic stress scenario. The objective was to implement each step rather than import it, because the textbook tells you which model to use and only the fitting tells you why.

## Why these calls and not the obvious ones

<TradeOffs>

<TradeOff chose="Gompertz-Makeham" over="plain Gompertz">

Gompertz models mortality rising exponentially with age and fits well in populations where most deaths are age-driven. Makeham adds a constant term for hazards that do not care how old you are.

In this region that term is not a refinement, it is a material share of the deaths. Fitting plain Gompertz to a young population with a high accident and infectious-disease baseline pushes the age-dependent parameters somewhere they should not be, and the model then misprices exactly the young lives that make up most of the book. I fitted it by maximum likelihood with a general-purpose optimiser rather than a canned actuarial package, which is where most of the learning in the project happened.

</TradeOff>

<TradeOff chose="an off-the-shelf library for Cox regression" over="implementing it from scratch too">

I implemented Gompertz-Makeham myself and did not implement Cox proportional hazards myself, and that inconsistency is deliberate.

Cox is a rabbit hole: partial likelihood, tie handling, baseline hazard estimation. Going down it would have been a different project with a different lesson, and the library implementation is solid and well tested. The learning I wanted was in mortality curve fitting and in the tail simulation. Deciding where to stop building is part of the work, and "implement everything from scratch" is a rule that quietly replaces the actual goal with a purity test.

</TradeOff>

<TradeOff chose="vectorised NumPy" over="the pandas loop that worked">

The first Monte Carlo iterated over 5,000 scenarios in a DataFrame and took four minutes a run. Identical results in NumPy take eight seconds.

Four minutes is not slow enough to be obviously broken, which is what makes it expensive. It is slow enough that you stop re-running it, and once you stop re-running it you stop checking whether a change to the mortality model moved the tail. The speedup did not buy compute. It bought iteration.

</TradeOff>

<TradeOff chose="age-band-specific pandemic multipliers" over="one flat shock factor">

The first stress test multiplied all mortality by 2.5. Simple, defensible-sounding, and wrong in the direction that matters most for an insurer.

Excess-mortality data from the recent pandemic concentrates sharply at older ages. A flat multiplier therefore overstates claims on a young book and understates the tail on an old one, which is precisely the calculation the stress test exists to get right. Recalibrating to roughly 1.5 at ages 30 to 50 and 3.5 above 70 changed the shape of the loss distribution, not just its mean.

</TradeOff>

</TradeOffs>

## What broke

<PostMortem
  title="A concordance of 0.62 was the model reporting a violated assumption, not a bad fit"
  symptom="The first Cox proportional hazards fit scored 0.62 on concordance, well under the 0.7 that counts as usable. My assumption was under-fitting or a bad covariate set, and I went looking for more features."
  cause="Proportional hazards assumes the ratio of hazards between two individuals is constant over time. Risk class and age interact: the marginal effect of being a smoker at 25 is not the effect at 65. With no interaction term the model was forced to fit one average ratio across ages, and it satisfied neither end of the range."
  fix="Add the age by risk-class interaction. Concordance moved from 0.62 to 0.77 on one term. Schoenfeld residual checks are now part of every fit rather than something I did afterwards."
  cost="Time spent hunting for missing data when the data was fine and the specification was wrong."
  lesson="A low score on a model with a named assumption is usually the assumption talking. The instinct to add features first meant I was treating a diagnostic as a scoreboard, and the diagnostic was more specific than I gave it credit for."
/>

The subtler error was in the survival fit itself. My first version ignored left truncation, delayed entry into the risk pool, which biases the estimated hazard downward because people are only observed after surviving long enough to be observed. Censoring and truncation are the part of survival analysis that looks like bookkeeping and is actually the modelling.

<Note>

The gap I would close first is reserves. Given the current book and this mortality model, what is the incurred-but-not-reported estimate? That is the actuary's daily work and it is not here. Neither is a sum-at-risk analogue to loss given default.

And as with the credit scorecard, the profiles are synthetic, calibrated to published regional health statistics. That is the honest second-best when no African insurer will share individual-level data, and it cannot contain an interaction I did not think to build into it.

</Note>
