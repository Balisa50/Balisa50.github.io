Banks across West Africa hold the data that could power local AI, and they cannot share it. Privacy law, customer trust and plain competitive advantage keep loan books on bank servers. That is the obvious problem. The harder one sits underneath it: for rural borrowers and the informal economy, much of the data was never collected anywhere, by anyone. There is nothing to anonymise.

So NOVA does two things. In Copy mode a Conditional Tabular GAN learns a dataset you already have and produces rows that match its structure without reproducing anyone in it. In Create mode you describe the columns, the distributions and the domain rules, and it generates records from nothing at all. The second mode is the one that matters for the populations that have no data, and it is not a GAN job. It needs a rule engine.

Every number below is measured on the shipped model. The validation is the project.

## Why these calls and not the obvious ones

<TradeOffs>

<TradeOff chose="CTGAN implemented from the paper in PyTorch" over="the CTGAN that ships in the Synthetic Data Vault">

The SDV version works and would have saved a week. It would also have been a box I could not open. Mode-specific normalisation for multimodal continuous columns, the conditional generator with its cross-entropy term, the gradient penalty, training-by-sampling: those are the four ideas that make tabular GANs work at all, and I wanted to be able to explain each of them under questioning.

The practical payoff came later. Because the model was mine, I could add a second generation path the library could never have given me.

</TradeOff>

<TradeOff chose="a rule engine for the seven financial domains" over="training seven separate CTGANs, one per domain">

The brief asked for seven models. That is roughly a day of CPU training, seven checkpoints to version, seven things to break, and a weaker story. Worse, it answers the wrong question: seven GANs still need seven real datasets to learn from, and the whole point was the case where you have none.

The domains became presets of the criteria engine instead. A user can now define an eighth, or describe rural Gambian borrower records, without me training anything.

</TradeOff>

<TradeOff chose="a structural-causal ground truth" over="independent column draws with a correlation matrix forced on top">

If every column is sampled independently and the relationships are stamped on afterwards, the data is incoherent, and validating that a GAN preserved the correlations becomes meaningless because there was nothing real to preserve. So the relationships come from shared latent drivers: education and age load onto income, income onto loan size, risk drivers onto a calibrated default logit.

Ten target correlations and seven integrity constraints are checked on every generation, and the generator fails loudly if a change breaks one.

</TradeOff>

<TradeOff chose="four validation metrics" over="the single headline number most write-ups report">

Shape, relationships, usefulness and safety are four different questions, and a model can pass one while failing another. Matching every marginal tells you nothing about whether the correlations survived. Being useful for prediction tells you nothing about whether a row sits too close to a real person.

Watching the four disagree is what stopped me hunting for one number.

</TradeOff>

</TradeOffs>

## What broke

<PostMortem
  title="The privacy metric was measuring the opposite of privacy"
  symptom="The privacy score came back at about 0.99. I read that as a catastrophic privacy failure and nearly published it as one."
  cause="The metric was a real-versus-synthetic detector: can a classifier tell generated rows from real ones. That is a fidelity measure, not a privacy measure, and it points the wrong way. A generator that simply memorised its training set would be perfectly undetectable and maximally unsafe. My most dangerous possible model would have scored best."
  fix="Replaced it with distance to closest record, which asks whether synthetic rows sit abnormally close to real training rows. The shipped model scores 1.10 with 1.1 percent near-duplicates. The detector stayed on as a fidelity diagnostic, where it belongs, and it reports 0.89 against a 0.50 baseline."
  cost="Nearly shipped a number that was both wrong and wrong in the flattering direction."
  lesson="A metric is a claim about what you are measuring, and that claim needs checking before the value does. Twice on this project I changed the metric rather than the model, and both times the new number looked worse and meant more."
/>

The same pattern caught the first fidelity run. It reported a 4 percent statistical pass rate, which looked like total failure. At n equals 10,000 a Kolmogorov-Smirnov p-value collapses to near zero for differences far too small to matter; the field scores the KS statistic instead. Real fidelity was 0.94 the whole time.

<Note>

The gaps are at the edges and worth stating. The backend runs on a free trial machine that sleeps after five minutes, so a cold start reads as a slow response. Distance to closest record shows no memorisation empirically, but it is not a formal guarantee, and a differentially private training option is what the strong claim would need. The transaction data here is tabular whereas real fraud is sequential, which is why I read TimeGAN before deciding not to claim that domain yet.

</Note>
